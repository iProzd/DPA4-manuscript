\section{Introduction}

Machine-learning interatomic potentials (MLIPs) are undergoing a paradigm shift, from case-specific MLIPs trained on dedicated density-functional-theory (DFT) data to solve narrow scientific problems, to large pretrained atomistic foundation models~\cite{yuan2026foundation}, also called large atomistic models (LAMs), that serve as universal DFT surrogates for molecular simulation, materials discovery and molecular design~\cite{merchant2023scaling}.
The architectural lineage mirrors this shift: the Behler--Parrinello neural-network potential~\cite{behler2007generalized}, Gaussian approximation potentials~\cite{bartok2010gaussian}, SchNet~\cite{schutt2017schnet}, PhysNet~\cite{unke2019physnet} and Deep Potential~\cite{zhang2018deep,wang2018deepmd} characterise the case-specific era, while M3GNet~\cite{chen2022universal}, CHGNet~\cite{deng2023chgnet}, MACE~\cite{batatia2022mace,kovacs2023mace}, MatterSim~\cite{yang2024mattersim}, Orb~\cite{neumann2024orb,rhodes2025orbv3}, UMA~\cite{wood2025umafamilyuniversalmodels} and the DPA series~\cite{zhang2022dpa,zhang2024dpa2,zhang2025dpa3} characterise the LAM era.


Although LAMs have demonstrated their potential to revolutionise materials and molecular design, training such models is prohibitively expensive: UMA-medium~\cite{wood2025umafamilyuniversalmodels}, built on the eSEN architecture~\cite{fu2025esen}, required 129{,}024 H200 GPU-hours to train, posing a substantial barrier to both training and downstream use.
This motivates the question whether comparable accuracy can be reached at substantially lower training cost.
% The next challenge is no longer accuracy alone, but the joint optimisation of accuracy, efficiency and physical robustness.
On the accuracy side, equivariant architectures carry directional information as first-class features instead of compressing it immediately into invariant scalars; NequIP~\cite{batzner2022nequip}, MACE~\cite{batatia2022mace}, the Equiformer family~\cite{liao2022equiformer,liao2023equiformerv2,liao2026equiformerv3} and eSEN~\cite{fu2025esen} have shown that this substantially improves data efficiency and benchmark accuracy.
On the efficiency side, the most expressive SO(3)-equivariant models rely on Clebsch--Gordan tensor products or dense spherical-grid operations whose cost grows rapidly with angular order, model width and neighbour count.
% This cost becomes a bottleneck when the target is not a single specialised potential, but an MLIP architecture that can scale to broad training sets and serve as the backbone of future LAMs.
Reducing SO(3) convolutions to edge-local SO(2) operations provides one route to this trade-off~\cite{passaro2023reducing}, exploited by recent high-performing models such as eSEN~\cite{fu2025esen} and EquiformerV3~\cite{liao2026equiformerv3}.
In these constructions, edge information is often introduced through invariant radial or scalar channels, whereas utilizing more expressive edge--node interactions require more intensive algibraic operations in the equivariant multiplication in the residual SO(2) basis.

Beyond architectural cost, a practical constraint shapes the training strategy of these models.
Conservative-force training is difficult to accelerate because the force loss differentiates through the energy model, so mainstream LLM training stacks, optimised for first-derivative gradients, do not transfer directly to this second-derivative setting.
Most leading models, such as EquiformerV3, eSEN and UMA, work around this by pre-training with denoising (DeNS~\cite{liao2024dens}) or direct-force prediction, which predict the denoising target or the atomic force in a single forward pass and thereby avoid the backpropagation pass required by conservative-force training, and only later fine-tune with the conservative-force objective.
This two-stage protocol adds substantial engineering complexity to LAM training, motivating an architecture in which conservative-force training is itself compile-friendly from the first step.
% These considerations motivate an architecture that treats directional expressiveness, and training efficiency as coupled design requirements rather than separate add-ons.

Here we present DPA4, an equivariant interatomic-potential architecture designed around these three requirements.
DPA4 combines local-frame equivariant message passing, compiler-friendly conservative-force training and native analytical short-range coupling in one model family.
DPA4 is a Architectural innovations co-designed for efficiency and accuracy: it combines (A1) a low-rank edge--node SO(2)-equivariant product in an edge-local frame, (A2) a multi-focus design for message non-linearity, (A3) envelope-gated attention for message aggregation, and (A4) a Lebedev-grid projection for SO(3)-equivariant non-linearity.
Together, A1--A3 raise the model's generalisation accuracy at low computational cost relative to standard SO(2)-equivariant baselines, and A4 maintains SO(3)-equivariance of the non-linearity to machine precision.
On the practical side, DPA4 enables a end-to-end \texttt{torch.compile} training.
DPA4 uses a shape-stable and compiler-friendly implementation of conservative force training.
This makes the energy-to-force path compatible with \texttt{torch.compile} and provides up to a 3.1$\times$ wall-clock training speedup in controlled ablations.
In addition, the short-range region of the potential-energy surface is sparsely sampled in ordinary DFT datasets; external Ziegler--Biersack--Littmark (ZBL) corrections can repair the asymptotic repulsion~\cite{ziegler1985}, but native and smooth coupling is preferable when the model is used in molecular dynamics.

        

DPA4 variants form a new accuracy--efficiency frontier on Matbench Discovery~\cite{riebesell2025matbench}, as summarized by the Combined Performance Score (CPS) versus training-cost comparison in Fig.~\ref{fig:cps}, and improve the molecular benchmark errors on SPICE-MACE-OFF~\cite{kovacs2023mace}.
The largest DPA4 variant reaches state-of-the-art Matbench Discovery performance, while smaller variants approach the accuracy of much larger baselines with substantially fewer parameters and lower training cost.
Matbench Discovery and SPICE-MACE-OFF are widely used benchmarks that respectively test inorganic-crystal relaxation and molecular force-field accuracy across organic chemistry, while avoiding validation dominated by strongly out-of-domain data or massive pretraining cost.
We establish DPA4 here as a generalist backbone in the single-task, per-dataset setting, spanning both inorganic crystals and organic molecules with one architecture; its use as the backbone for multi-task large atomistic model (LAM) pretraining---the route to a fully pretrained foundation model---is discussed as the natural next step in Section~\ref{sec:discussion}.

The remainder of the paper is organized as follows.
Section~\ref{sec:results} first gives an overview of the DPA4 architecture, then reports its accuracy on Matbench Discovery and SPICE-MACE-OFF, its training and inference efficiency, its native ZBL coupling behaviour, and controlled ablations of the main architectural and systems components.
Section~\ref{sec:discussion} discusses the implications and limitations of these results.
Section~\ref{sec:methods} describes the architecture, datasets, training protocol and compiled conservative-force implementation in detail.
